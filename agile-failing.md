# How Agile Goes Wrong

Agile fails more often than it succeeds in the wild — not
because the ideas are bad, but because organizations adopt
the vocabulary and keep the old habits. The failure modes
have names.

## Cargo-cult agile

Doing the ceremonies — standups, sprints, retros, Jira
boards — without the thinking behind them. The team holds a
daily standup where everyone reports status to a manager.
Sprints are just two-week deadlines. Retros produce action
items nobody does. It *looks* like agile from a distance,
which is exactly the problem: the resemblance replaces the
substance.

**Test:** ask "what did we change because of our last
retro?" If the answer is nothing, three retros running,
you have cargo-cult agile.

## Scrum-but

"We do Scrum, *but* we don't do retros / *but* the Product
Owner is a committee / *but* sprints are really just
deadlines." Every *but* removes a load-bearing wall. Some
adaptations are legitimate; most are the old command
structure reasserting itself in agile clothing.

## Velocity as a performance metric

Management discovers velocity, then weaponizes it: "Team A
does 40 points, why does Team B do 25?" Points inflate
overnight. Estimation becomes negotiation theater. The
number that was useful for forecasting becomes useless for
everything, because Goodhart's law eats metrics for
breakfast: *when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to
be a good measure.*

## Agile as micromanagement theater

The daily scrum becomes a daily status interrogation. The
board becomes surveillance — "why has that ticket been in
progress for two days?" Burndown charts become tools for
asking why the line isn't steeper. All the transparency
agile creates gets aimed *downward* at the team instead of
*outward* at the work. Developers learn to game the
visibility instead of doing the work.

## The Agile Industrial Complex

Certifications, coaches, consultants, and frameworks sold
as the thing itself. A two-day Certified ScrumMaster course
does not make someone able to run a team, and an
organization that buys SAFe hoping process will substitute
for trust will get neither. Process can't fix a lack of
trust or unclear authority — it can only make the
dysfunction more legible.

## "Agile" as an excuse for chaos

The opposite failure: "we're agile, so we don't plan,
don't document, don't estimate." That's not agile; that's
abdication. The manifesto values *responding to change* —
which requires knowing what the plan was, so you can tell
you've deviated from it.

## The common root

Nearly every failure mode is the same story: the
organization wanted the *benefits* of agile (faster
delivery, happier customers) without the *costs* (giving
teams real authority, accepting uncertainty in forecasts,
managers becoming servant-leaders instead of directors).
Agile requires trust to work, and you can't install trust
with a framework.
