# What Is Agile?

In 2001, seventeen software developers met at a ski resort in
Utah and wrote the **Agile Manifesto** — four values and
twelve principles that said, roughly: *the old way of
planning everything up front isn't working; here's what
we've seen work instead.*

## The four values

> **Individuals and interactions** over processes and tools
>
> **Working software** over comprehensive documentation
>
> **Customer collaboration** over contract negotiation
>
> **Responding to change** over following a plan

Read them carefully: the items on the right aren't
worthless — the manifesto says "while there is value in the
items on the right, we value the items on the left more."
Agile done badly treats the right side as the enemy. It
isn't. Nobody wants *broken* software with no documentation
built by people who never talk.

## The core idea in one paragraph

Build a small piece. Show it to someone who cares. Learn
what's wrong. Build the next small piece better. Repeat.
Everything in agile — sprints, standups, backlogs, boards —
is scaffolding around that loop. If a practice doesn't
shorten the loop between *building* and *learning*, it's
not pulling its weight.

## What agile is not

- **Not "no planning."** Agile plans constantly — in small
  increments, with fresh information. "No planning" is just
  chaos with a trendy name.
- **Not "no documentation."** It's *enough* documentation,
  written when it's useful instead of up front when it's
  speculative.
- **Not a specific process.** Scrum and Kanban are
  frameworks people use *with* agile thinking. Doing the
  ceremonies without the thinking is the most common way
  agile fails (see [How Agile Goes Wrong](agile-failing.md)).
- **Not faster in the way managers hope.** Agile doesn't
  make work take less time. It makes *wrong* work visible
  sooner, so you waste less of it.

## Where it came from

Before agile, the dominant model was **waterfall**: gather
all requirements, design everything, build everything, test
everything — in sequence, over months or years. It works
when requirements genuinely don't change (building a bridge;
launching a rocket). Software requirements *always* change,
because building the thing teaches you what the thing
should be. Agile is the admission that learning is part of
the work, not a failure of planning.
