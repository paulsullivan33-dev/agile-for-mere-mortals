# Agile for Small Teams

Everything so far assumed a team. But agile's best ideas
survive contact with a team of one — or three — if you keep
the parts that earn their keep and drop the ceremony.

## The solo developer's agile

You are the Product Owner, the Scrum Master, and the team.
What survives:

- **A backlog.** One ordered list of "what's next." The
  discipline isn't the tool — it's that there's *one* list
  and the top is always the next thing. A text file works.
- **Small batches.** Finish something shippable regularly.
  The build-learn loop doesn't need a sprint boundary; it
  needs you to stop polishing and show the thing to
  someone.
- **A personal retro.** Every week or two, ten minutes:
  what slowed me down? What will I change? Write it down —
  memory lies about process the way it lies about
  everything.
- **WIP limit of one (or two).** Finish before starting.
  The solo developer's biggest enemy is eleven
  half-finished branches.

What to drop: standups (talk to the dog instead), story
points (you know how fast you are), planning poker (you'll
agree with yourself suspiciously often).

## The 2–5 person team

Keep it lighter than Scrum-by-the-book:

- **A board** (Kanban-style, even if you sprint). Columns
  match your actual workflow; WIP limits stay.
- **A weekly planning moment.** Not a two-hour ceremony —
  thirty minutes deciding what's next and who's doing what.
- **A demo habit.** Show working software regularly, even
  if the "stakeholder" is each other. Demos kill
  "almost done" as a permanent state.
- **A retro that changes things.** Small teams can act on
  retro items immediately — that's the advantage; use it.
- **One prioritizer.** Even of three people, one person
  breaks ties on what matters most. "We decide together"
  works until it doesn't, usually at the worst moment.

## What "done" means (write it down)

Small teams skip this and pay for it forever. A shared,
written **definition of done** — tested, reviewed,
documented, deployed, whatever your bar is — ends more
arguments than any framework. Tape it to the wall (or the
top of the backlog file).

## The one practice that matters most

If you keep exactly one agile practice, keep the
**retrospective** — the regular, honest look at how the
work is going and one change to try. Iterating on the
*product* is agile; iterating on *how you work* is what
makes it compound. Teams that never examine their process
get the same problems forever, just faster.
